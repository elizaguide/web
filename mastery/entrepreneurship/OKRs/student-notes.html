1. Foundations of OKRs

What are OKRs?

OKR stands for Objectives and Key Results, a goal-setting framework that helps organizations define what they want to achieve and how they'll measure success.

Core Definition

Objectives: Qualitative, inspirational goals describing the direction you want to move

Key Results: Quantifiable, measurable outcomes that define what success looks like for each objective

Brief History

  • 1954: Peter Drucker introduces Management by Objectives (MBO) at General Motors
  • 1975: Andy Grove refines the concept at Intel as "OKRs"
  • 1999: John Doerr introduces OKRs to Google as a small startup with ~40 employees
  • 2000s: Rapid adoption by Google, Meta, LinkedIn, Twitter, Airbnb
  • 2010s-2020s: Widespread adoption across industries and company sizes

Why OKRs Matter

  • Alignment: Ensures entire organization works toward shared goals
  • Focus: Forces prioritization of what truly matters
  • Accountability: Clear metrics make progress visible and measurable
  • Agility: Quarterly cycles allow rapid adaptation
  • Motivation: Ambitious goals inspire teams to achieve more
  • Learning: Regular reviews build organizational intelligence
💡 Key Insight: OKRs aren't about hitting 100% of targets. They're about setting ambitious goals (60-70% achievable) and learning from what you achieve or fail to achieve.

2. Anatomy of an OKR

The Structure

Each OKR consists of:

Objective

What: A qualitative, inspirational statement

Characteristics:

  • Action-oriented and inspiring
  • Directional (not a number)
  • Memorable and motivating
  • Usually 1 sentence

Example: "Become the most trusted fitness platform"

Key Results (typically 3-4)

What: Quantifiable, measurable outcomes that define success

Characteristics:

  • Starts with a measurable verb (increase, reduce, achieve)
  • Has a baseline and a target
  • Objective and trackable
  • Outcome-focused, not activity-focused

Example: "Increase daily active users from 100K to 500K"

Full OKR Example

Product Team - Q2 OKRs

Objective: Deliver a world-class mobile experience

Key Results:

  1. Improve app performance: Reduce average load time from 3s to 1s
  2. Increase user engagement: Grow weekly active users from 2M to 4M
  3. Improve retention: Reduce 30-day churn from 8% to 4%
  4. Achieve product-market fit: Achieve Net Promoter Score (NPS) of 60+

Objectives vs Key Results

Aspect Objective Key Result
Type Qualitative Quantitative
Inspiration Inspirational & directional Measurable & specific
Format A statement A metric with target
Count Usually 3-5 per cycle 3-4 per objective
Example Dominate the enterprise market Acquire 50 enterprise customers

3. Setting Effective OKRs

Characteristics of Good OKRs

  • Aspirational: Should excite your team and feel like a stretch
  • Achievable: Realistic with maximum effort (60-70% success rate)
  • Measurable: Success is objectively defined and trackable
  • Focused: 3-5 company OKRs, 3-4 per team (not 20+)
  • Time-bound: Quarterly cycles keep momentum
  • Transparent: Visible to entire organization
  • Outcome-focused: Measures business impact, not activity

The 60-70 Rule

Why 60-70% achievable?

Below 60%: Goals feel impossible, team loses motivation

60-70%: Goals are ambitious but achievable with great effort

Above 80%: Goals are too easy, not stretching the team

The Math: If you're hitting 100% of your OKRs, they're not ambitious enough. Plan to achieve 0.7 on average.

OKR Setting Process

Step 1: Define Company Purpose

Why does your company exist? What are you trying to accomplish in the world?

Step 2: Leadership OKRs

Company leadership defines 3-5 strategic OKRs for the quarter that align with the mission.

Step 3: Team Proposals

Each team proposes OKRs that support company goals and align with their domain.

Step 4: Review & Alignment

Leadership reviews team proposals and ensures alignment across the organization.

Step 5: Commit & Communicate

Finalize OKRs and communicate them company-wide with clarity on why they matter.

Outcome vs Output

Output (Activity) Outcome (Impact)
Launch 5 new features Increase user engagement from 2h to 3h daily
Hire 20 engineers Reduce feature development time from 3 months to 1 month
Run 10 marketing campaigns Increase qualified leads from 100 to 500 per month
Improve documentation Reduce customer support tickets from 50 to 20 weekly
💡 Key Insight: Focus on outcomes (what changed in the business) not outputs (what activities you did).

4. Tracking and Scoring OKRs

The Scoring Scale

OKRs are scored on a scale of 0.0 to 1.0 at the end of each quarter:

Score Status Interpretation
0.0 - 0.3 Off Track Significant issues or blockers. Major course correction needed.
0.4 - 0.6 On Track (some challenges) Making progress but facing headwinds. Some course correction may help.
0.7 - 1.0 Achieved/Exceeded Goal met or exceeded. Strong execution and good prioritization.

Healthy Target Score

Average Company Score Should Be ~0.7

Score of 1.0: Your OKRs were too easy. Next quarter, make them more ambitious.

Score of 0.7: Perfect. You achieved what you set out to do with great effort.

Score of 0.5: You had significant challenges. Debug what went wrong and adjust.

Why not 1.0? If everyone achieves 100% of goals, you're not thinking big enough. Stretch goals are healthy.

Weekly Check-Ins

Don't wait until the end of the quarter to assess progress. Weekly check-ins are critical:

  • Every Monday or Friday, teams update progress on each Key Result
  • Status: Green (on track), Yellow (some challenges), Red (off track)
  • Brief update: "What changed? What blockers appeared?"
  • Action: "What are we doing about it?"

Scoring Key Results

Example: "Increase Monthly Revenue from $100K to $500K"

Target: $500K (5x growth)

Possible outcomes:

  • $700K achieved → Score 1.0 (exceeded)
  • $500K achieved → Score 1.0 (hit target)
  • $400K achieved → Score 0.8 (80% of target)
  • $300K achieved → Score 0.6 (60% of target)
  • $150K achieved → Score 0.3 (30% of target)

5. Cascading OKRs

Top-Down and Bottom-Up

OKRs work best when they combine strategic planning with operational input:

🔺 Top-Down (Leadership)

CEO and leadership define 3-5 company-wide OKRs that set strategic direction for the quarter.

Example Company OKR: "Become the market leader in SaaS productivity tools"

🔻 Bottom-Up (Teams)

Each team proposes OKRs aligned to company goals but reflecting their unique domain and capabilities.

Example Product Team OKR: "Deliver the fastest, most reliable platform"

🤝 Alignment & Negotiation

Leadership reviews team proposals and facilitates discussions to ensure company-wide coherence and feasibility.

Example Cascade

Company Level

Objective: Dominate the enterprise market

Key Results:

  1. Acquire 100 enterprise customers (vs. 20 last year)
  2. Achieve $50M ARR (vs. $10M last year)
  3. Improve NPS from 40 to 65

Sales Team OKRs (supporting company goals)

Objective: Become the go-to enterprise sales force

Key Results:

  1. Close 80 net new enterprise deals (vs. 15 last year)
  2. Achieve $8M ARR from enterprise (vs. $2M last year)
  3. Reduce sales cycle from 6 months to 3 months

Product Team OKRs (supporting company goals)

Objective: Build enterprise-grade features

Key Results:

  1. Launch 5 enterprise features (SSO, audit logs, etc.)
  2. Achieve 99.99% uptime SLA
  3. Increase enterprise customer CSAT to 95%

Levels of OKRs

  • Company OKRs: Strategic direction set by leadership (3-5 OKRs)
  • Department OKRs: How departments support company goals (3-5 per department)
  • Team OKRs: How individual teams drive outcomes (3-4 per team)
  • Individual Goals: Optional - what individuals work on (not always as OKRs)
💡 Key Insight: Not all top-level OKRs need to be supported by team OKRs. Some are owned entirely by leadership. And teams may have OKRs that don't directly ladder up to company goals.

6. Best Practices

Frequency: Quarterly Cycles

OKRs work best on a quarterly cadence:

  • Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 align with business calendar
  • Long enough to make meaningful progress (too short = noise)
  • Short enough to adapt quickly (too long = rigid, less agile)
  • Allows for learning and continuous improvement

Typical OKR Cycle Timeline

Week Activity
Week -2 Leadership defines draft company OKRs for next quarter
Week -1 Teams propose OKRs aligned to company direction
Week 0 Review, alignment discussions, final OKRs committed
Weeks 1-13 Weekly check-ins, progress tracking, course corrections
Week 13 End-of-quarter scoring and grading
Week 14 Retrospective: What did we learn? How do we improve?

Transparency is Key

  • Publish OKRs company-wide (not secret)
  • Create a shared OKR tracking document (Google Sheets, Ally, 15Five, etc.)
  • Weekly updates on progress visible to everyone
  • Psychological safety: Sharing struggles is encouraged, not punished

Separate OKRs from Compensation

  • OKRs should NOT be directly tied to bonuses or promotions
  • Why? Because it removes psychological safety and kills risk-taking
  • Use OKRs for strategy, other metrics for compensation decisions
  • Exception: For very senior leaders, OKR achievement may influence bonuses (but not all compensation)

Focus on Outcomes, Not Activities

Wrong (Activity-Focused)

✗ "Launch 5 new features"

✗ "Hire 20 engineers"

✗ "Run 10 marketing campaigns"

Right (Outcome-Focused)

✓ "Increase daily active users 2x"

✓ "Reduce feature development time by 50%"

✓ "Increase qualified leads from 100 to 500/month"

Build in Flexibility

  • Quarterly OKRs shouldn't be completely rigid
  • Allow mid-quarter pivots if market conditions change dramatically
  • Teams can adjust Key Results if circumstances warrant (e.g., major client loss)
  • But don't use this as an excuse to change goals constantly

7. Common OKR Mistakes

  • Setting too many OKRs: More than 5 company OKRs or more than 4 per team dilutes focus
  • Making them too easy: 100% achievement rate means they're not ambitious enough
  • Confusing output with outcome: "Launch features" vs "Increase user engagement"
  • Tying OKRs directly to compensation: Kills psychological safety and risk-taking
  • Never revising mid-quarter: OKRs shouldn't be completely rigid in a changing world
  • Lack of transparency: Secret OKRs prevent alignment and motivation
  • No weekly check-ins: You can't wait until end-of-quarter to assess progress
  • Cascading every single OKR: Some company OKRs don't need team-level OKRs
  • Forgetting the retrospective: Learning from failures is the whole point
  • Treating OKRs as annual goals: Quarterly cycles are essential for agility
💡 Key Insight: The most common mistake is making OKRs too easy. If you're hitting 95%+ achievement, you're not thinking big enough.

9. Implementation Guide

Phase 1: Foundation (Month 1)

  • Educate leadership on OKR framework
  • Define company mission and strategic priorities
  • Choose an OKR tracking tool (Google Sheets, Ally, 15Five, etc.)
  • Draft company-level OKRs for next quarter

Phase 2: Cascading (Month 2)

  • Communicate company OKRs to all teams
  • Have teams propose OKRs aligned to company goals
  • Leadership reviews and provides feedback
  • Finalize OKRs through alignment discussions

Phase 3: Execution (Months 2-4)

  • All-hands meeting to communicate OKRs and why they matter
  • Weekly team check-ins on progress
  • Monthly all-hands reviews of company progress
  • Mid-quarter pivot discussions if needed

Phase 4: Review & Learning (Month 4)

  • Score OKRs at end of quarter (0.0-1.0)
  • Hold retrospectives: What did we learn? What went well? What didn't?
  • Celebrate wins and acknowledge challenges
  • Use learnings to inform next quarter's OKRs

Recommended OKR Tools

  • 15Five: Full-featured OKR tracking, popular with enterprises
  • Ally: Lightweight, collaborative OKR platform
  • Google Sheets: Free, simple, works for small teams
  • Lattice: Performance management with OKRs built in
  • GForce: Custom OKR tracking for enterprises

Quick Reference: OKR Writing Checklist

  • Does it start with a strong action verb?
  • Is it measurable and quantified?
  • Does it have a baseline and target?
  • Is it ambitious but achievable (60-70% success rate)?
  • Can progress be tracked weekly?
  • Is it outcome-focused, not activity-focused?
  • Would achieving this create business value?
  • Does it fit in 1 sentence?
